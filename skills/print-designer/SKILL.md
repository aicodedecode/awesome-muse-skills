## Overview

Print is unforgiving: what you see on screen is not what comes off the press, mistakes cost real
money, and there's no "deploy a fix." Professional print design means understanding color
management, resolution, bleeds, paper, and prepress — the physical realities between your file and
the finished piece. This skill covers designing for print from concept to press-ready files.

## When to use

- Designing brochures, flyers, posters, or business cards

- Preparing books, magazines, or catalogs for print

- Creating large-format graphics (banners, signage, trade show)

- Understanding paper stocks, finishes, and binding

- Troubleshooting print production issues

## Core concepts

- - **CMYK is the reality.** Screens are RGB (light); print is CMYK (ink). Bright RGB blues and
  greens literally cannot be printed — design in CMYK or convert early and adjust. Always soft-proof
  critical colors.
- - **Resolution: 300 DPI at final size.** The non-negotiable for offset print. Large format is the
  exception (viewing distance scales: a billboard at 10 DPI viewed from 100 feet looks sharp —
  calculate by viewing distance).
- - **Bleed, trim, safe.** Bleed (artwork extends 3mm/0.125" past trim), trim (the cut line), safe
  area (keep critical content 5mm+ inside trim). Miss the bleed and you get white edges; miss the
  safe area and text gets cropped.
- - **Paper is a design material.** Uncoated (soft, absorbent — colors mute), coated/gloss (vibrant,
  sharp), textured stocks (tactile luxury). Paper choice changes how every color and image renders —
  spec it deliberately, request samples.
- - **Rich black vs plain black.** Large black areas in plain K (100K) look washed out; rich black
  (e.g., 60C/40M/40Y/100K) gives depth. But small text should stay 100K only (registration issues
  blur multi-plate small type).
- - **Finishes elevate.** Foil, embossing, spot UV, soft-touch laminate — used sparingly on the
  right piece, they signal premium. Used everywhere, they're expensive noise.

## Practical workflow

1. 1. **Know the specs first.** Final trim size, bleed requirements, color mode (CMYK? spot
   colors?), paper stock, binding method, quantity. Get the printer's spec sheet before designing —
   not after.
2. 2. **Set up the document correctly.** CMYK color mode, 300 DPI, correct bleed in document setup,
   facing pages for bound pieces, proper slug area for printer notes.
3. 3. **Design with print in mind.** Watch total ink coverage (stay under ~300% for coated stocks),
   avoid tiny reversed-out text (fills in on press), keep critical content in safe areas, design
   spreads accounting for the gutter/creep.
4. 4. **Handle images properly.** 300 DPI at placed size (check effective resolution, not just file
   resolution), CMYK conversion with profile, no RGB stragglers. Enlarge in the layout = resolution
   loss.
5. 5. **Typography for print.** Outlined or embedded fonts, no faux bolds, adequate sizes (7pt
   minimum for body, larger for uncoated stocks where ink spreads), proper hyphenation and
   justification settings.
6. 6. **Preflight everything.** Preflight check: color mode, resolution, bleeds, font embedding,
   spot color naming, overprint settings, total ink. Export PDF/X-1a or the printer's preferred
   standard.
7. 7. **Proof before the run.** Digital proof for content, hard proof (contract proof) for
   color-critical work. Check the proof like a stranger: read every word, check every image, verify
   pagination. Sign off deliberately.

## Common pitfalls

- - **RGB files to press.** Neon colors that die on conversion, discovered after printing 10,000
  copies. Convert and proof early.
- - **Missing bleed.** The #1 rookie error. White slivers on trimmed edges scream amateur and can't
  be fixed after cutting.
- - **Low-resolution images.** Web images (72 DPI) placed in print layouts. They look fine on screen
  at 25% zoom and terrible on paper.
- - **Small text in rich black or reversed.** Registration shifts turn tiny multi-plate text into
  blurry rainbows. Small type: 100K only.
- - **Ignoring creep and gutter.** Thick perfect-bound books: inner margins get swallowed. Account
  for creep (page shift) and generous gutters.
- - **No hard proof on color-critical jobs.** "It looked right on my monitor" — monitors aren't
  calibrated to presses. For brand colors and photography, proof on paper.
