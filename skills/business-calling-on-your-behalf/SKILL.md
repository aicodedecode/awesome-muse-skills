# Business Calling on Your Behalf

Many tasks stall in email threads and hold queues when one phone
call would settle them. This skill is the procedure for placing
that call well: prepared, transparent, and bounded.

## Hard bounds

- **Businesses only, at their published numbers.** This procedure
  is for businesses, offices, and support lines. Calling people
  personally is a different task with its own consent rules.
- **Transparently the assistant.** The caller introduces itself
  as the user's assistant calling on their behalf. Never
  impersonate the user or anyone else.
- **No payments, ever.** No card numbers spoken, no charges
  authorized, no payment-method changes by phone.
- **No new commitments unless the user ordered them.** Asking
  questions, confirming details, and rescheduling an existing
  appointment the user asked to move are in bounds. Creating a
  new booking, contract, or purchase needs the user's explicit
  instruction first.
- **Share only what the brief approves.** Names, times, and
  identifiers go in the brief because the user approved sharing
  them with this business. Nothing else gets volunteered.

## Before the call

1. Confirm the call is the right next step: the question cannot
   be answered from the user's records, email, or the business's
   website, or those lanes already failed.
2. Verify the number is the business's own published number and
   note its listed name and address, so the destination can be
   checked before dialing.
3. Build the brief, and show it to the user when the call is not
   one they already specified exactly:
   - The business and number.
   - The one-sentence goal of the call.
   - The exact questions to ask, in order.
   - The facts the business may ask for that the user approved
     sharing (name, appointment time, order or account reference).
   - What success looks like, and what to do if the answer is a
     voicemail, a transfer, or a callback offer.
4. Get the user's go-ahead for this specific call: this business,
   this goal, these questions. A general "handle it" for a task
   covers the calls the task obviously needs; a new kind of call
   gets its own confirmation.

## During the call

- Introduce the call honestly: assistant, on behalf of the user,
  with the reason for calling in one sentence.
- Work the question list in order. Ask one thing at a time and
  note each answer as it comes.
- If the business asks for something outside the brief (a payment,
  a new commitment, information the user did not approve
  sharing), decline politely, note the request, and end or park
  the call there. Never improvise past the brief.
- If transferred or put on hold beyond a reasonable wait, note
  who or what department was reached and any callback number or
  reference given.

## After the call

1. Report per question: asked, answered (with the answer), or not
   reached. Distinguish what the business said from what is
   confirmed in writing anywhere.
2. Record the outcome where the task lives: the date and time of
   the call, the business, the answers, and any reference numbers,
   so the call never has to be repeated to recover them.
3. Name the follow-up the call created (a callback expected, a
   confirmation email promised, a decision the user now owns) and
   who owns it.
4. A call that failed to connect is reported as exactly that,
   with the next step proposed. Never retry in a loop; one redial
   is reasonable, repeated dialing is not.

## When a task should become a call

- A support question that needs a human at the business and has
  no working self-service path.
- Confirming hours, stock, eligibility, or a document requirement
  before the user travels or submits something.
- Rescheduling or confirming an existing appointment.
- Chasing a promised refund, delivery, or callback that has gone
  quiet past its promised date.

Email stays the better lane when a paper trail matters more than
speed, or when the business is known to answer email reliably.
