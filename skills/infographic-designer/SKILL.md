## Overview

Infographics translate complexity into understanding — but the genre is polluted
with chart junk,
misleading visuals, and decoration masquerading as information. This skill is
about honest visual
storytelling: choosing the right chart for the data, structuring the narrative
flow, and designing
for comprehension first, shareability second.

## When to use

- Visualizing survey results, research findings, or statistics

- Explaining processes, timelines, or how-things-work

- Creating shareable data content for marketing or social

- Turning a dense report into a visual summary

- Reviewing an infographic for accuracy and clarity

## Core concepts

- - - **Data honesty is sacred.** Truncated axes, cherry-picked ranges, area
  distortions, and
  misleading pictographs destroy trust. Every visual choice should survive the
question: "does this
  fairly represent the data?"
- - - **Right chart for the message.** Comparison → bars; trend over time →
  lines; part-to-whole →
  stacked bars (pie only for 2-3 parts); distribution → histograms; relationship
→ scatterplots.
  Novelty charts (3D pies, radial everything) usually obscure.
- - - **Narrative structure.** Headline finding → supporting evidence in logical
  order →
  methodology/source → takeaway. An infographic is a story, not a data dump;
sequence matters.
- - - **Hierarchy of numbers.** One hero stat (big, unforgettable), supporting
  stats (medium), context
  (small). Not every number deserves equal weight — design the importance.
- - - **Annotation over decoration.** Labels directly on data, callouts
  explaining the "so what,"
  source citations. Every decorative element should be interrogated: does it aid
understanding? If
  not, cut it.
- - - **Design for the channel.** A tall infographic for Pinterest/blog
  embedding, square or carousel
  for social, landscape for decks. One design rarely serves all — adapt the
layout per channel.

## Practical workflow

1. 1. 1. **Understand the data.** Read the source, check methodology, note
   caveats and sample sizes.
   You can't visualize honestly what you don't understand.
2. 2. 2. **Find the story.** What's the single most important finding? What's
   surprising? Structure 3-5
   supporting points that build the narrative. Kill interesting-but-irrelevant
data.
3. 3. 3. **Choose visual forms.** Match each point to its best chart or diagram
   type. Sketch the flow:
   how does the eye travel from headline to conclusion?
4. 4. 4. **Draft the layout.** Wireframe the sections: hero stat, supporting
   visuals in narrative
   order, methodology footer. Check the story reads top-to-bottom without the
designer's
   explanation.
5. 5. 5. **Design with restraint.** Limited palette (one accent for key data,
   neutrals for context),
   consistent chart styling, clear typography hierarchy. Data ink maximized,
decoration minimized.
6. 6. 6. **Verify everything.** Recheck every number against the source, every
   axis for honest scaling,
   every claim for fair representation. Have someone else fact-check — designers
go number-blind.
7. 7. 7. **Export for channels.** Full version for web/print, adapted crops for
   social, alt text
   describing the key findings for accessibility. Include the source link
wherever it's shared.

## Common pitfalls

- - - **Misleading visuals.** Truncated y-axes exaggerating small differences,
  pictograph areas
  scaling incorrectly, dual axes implying false correlations. When in doubt,
start axes at zero and
  label honestly.
- - - **Data vomit.** Cramming every available statistic in. An infographic with
  40 numbers
  communicates zero numbers. Curate to the story.
- - - **Decoration over information.** Illustrations, icons, and backgrounds
  that compete with the
  data. Every pixel should serve comprehension.
- - - **Missing sources.** No methodology, no sample size, no source link.
  Unsourced infographics are
  rumors with good design.
- - - **Unreadable at size.** Tiny labels, cramped charts, text that fails on
  mobile. Test at the
  actual viewing size for each channel.
- - - **The kitchen-sink timeline.** Timelines with 30 undifferentiated events.
  Highlight the 5 that
  matter; summarize or cut the rest.
