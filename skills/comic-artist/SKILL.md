## Overview

Comics are cinema on paper: the artist controls time (panel size and count), camera (angle and
framing), and sound (lettering and effects) — all in static images. Great comic art serves the
story's rhythm: knowing when to linger on a silent beat and when to accelerate through action. This
skill covers visual storytelling, panel craft, lettering, and production for print and web.

## When to use

- Creating comics, graphic novels, or manga

- Designing webcomics or comic strips

- Learning panel composition and page flow

- Improving lettering and dialogue presentation

- Preparing comics for print or digital publishing

## Core concepts

- - **Panel = time.** Big panels slow time (lingering moments, reveals); small panels accelerate
  (action, montage). Panel count per page sets the pace: 4-6 for standard flow, 1-2 for impact, 8+
  for dense or frantic sequences.
- - **Reading flow.** Western comics: left-to-right, top-to-bottom in a Z-path; manga:
  right-to-left. Panel arrangement must never confuse the reading order — when in doubt, simplify
  the grid. The reader should never wonder "what do I read next?"
- - **The gutter does work.** The space between panels is where readers infer action (closure). Use
  it deliberately: wide gutters pause, bleeding panels together accelerates, borderless panels feel
  open or dreamlike.
- - **Camera and staging.** Vary shot sizes for rhythm (establishing → medium → close-up for
  emotional beats), keep the 180-degree rule for dialogue, and stage action clearly — confusing
  action sequences are the #1 comic art failure.
- - **Lettering is half the art.** Balloon placement guides reading order, font choice sets tone,
  emphasis (bold/italics) directs the voice in the reader's head, and SFX are graphic design. Bad
  lettering ruins good art.
- - **Black and white thinking.** Even for color work: values (light/dark structure) carry
  readability. Squint at the page — if the focal points and flow aren't clear in grayscale, color
  won't fix it.

## Practical workflow

1. 1. **Write or break down the script.** Page-by-page beats: what happens, what changes, the
   emotional turn. Thumbnail the whole story first — pacing problems are cheapest to fix at
   thumbnail size.
2. 2. **Design the page layouts.** Vary panel counts for rhythm: quiet pages breathe (fewer, larger
   panels), action compresses (more, smaller). Plan page turns — reveals and cliffhangers belong on
   the right-hand page turn.
3. 3. **Pencil with staging priority.** Clear silhouettes, readable action, strong compositions.
   Check: can a stranger follow the action without dialogue? If not, restage.
4. 4. **Ink for clarity and mood.** Line weight variation (thicker outlines, thinner details),
   blacks for depth and focus, textures for atmosphere. Inking defines the comic's visual voice.
5. 5. **Letter professionally.** Place balloons in reading order (never crossing the art's focal
   point), leave breathing room, choose a legible comic font (or hand-letter consistently), size for
   the final print/digital dimensions.
6. 6. **Color with intent.** Establish the palette per scene (time of day, mood), use color for
   focus (saturated focal points, muted backgrounds), keep skin tones and key elements consistent
   across pages.
7. 7. **Prep for the medium.** Print: CMYK, bleeds, safe areas, correct page size. Web: RGB,
   readable at phone widths (consider vertical scroll format), file optimization. Webtoon-style
   vertical scroll is a different layout discipline — design for it natively.

## Common pitfalls

- - **Confusing action.** The reader can't tell what happened in a fight or chase. Slow down,
  clarify staging, use clear silhouettes — spectacle without clarity is noise.
- - **Talking heads.** Pages of identical medium shots with dialogue. Vary angles, use reactions and
  inserts, let the art carry subtext the words don't say.
- - **Lettering as afterthought.** Balloons covering faces, tiny text, inconsistent fonts. Letter
  during layout, not after coloring — balloon placement shapes composition.
- - **No page-turn craft.** Treating every page identically instead of using the physical turn for
  reveals and pacing. The page turn is comics' unique tool — use it.
- - **Inconsistent characters.** The protagonist looks different every chapter. Model sheets and
  reference discipline (see character-designer).
- - **Ignoring the format.** Designing print spreads for a vertical-scroll webcomic (or vice versa).
  The reading format shapes every layout decision — commit early.
