## Overview

Promotions are not rewards for tenure — they are decisions that you are already
operating at the
next level. Most promotion cases fail because they argue "I deserve it" instead
of proving "I'm
already doing it." This skill builds a packet that maps your work to the next
level's expectations,
with evidence a promotion committee can verify.

## When to use

- Preparing for a promotion cycle (6-12 months out or imminent)

- Writing a promotion packet or self-nomination document

- Deciding whether you're ready to go up for promotion

- Recovering from a denied promotion and planning the next attempt

- Helping a direct report build their case

## Core concepts

- - - **The level rubric is the exam paper.** Get the written expectations for
  the target level. Your
  packet is an answer key: every criterion gets 1-2 pieces of evidence. No
rubric, no case.
- - - **Scope is the currency.** Promotions hinge on expanded scope: larger
  projects, broader
  influence, mentoring others, driving decisions beyond your team. Show the
delta from your current
  level, not just good performance at it.
- - - **Sustained, not spiky.** One heroic quarter rarely suffices. Committees
  look for 2-3 quarters
  of consistent next-level performance. Start building the case early.
- - - **Sponsors, not just supporters.** You need people with influence in the
  room saying your name.
  Identify 2-3 senior stakeholders whose work you materially improved, and make
sure they know your
  promotion is on the table.
- - - **The "already operating" test.** For each next-level expectation, ask:
  "if I were already at
  this level, what would I be doing differently?" The gap between that answer
and your current work
  is your project list.
- - - **Denial is data.** If denied, get the specific gaps in writing within a
  week. Vague feedback
  ("need more visibility") is unactionable — push for the concrete behaviors
expected.

## Practical workflow

1. 1. 1. **Read the rubric.** Obtain the level definitions for current and
   target level. Highlight the
   4-6 differentiators — these are your packet's section headers.
2. 2. 2. **Run the gap analysis.** For each differentiator: evidence you have,
   evidence you lack, and a
   plan to close the gap in the next 1-2 quarters. Be brutally honest;
committees will be.
3. 3. 3. **Build the evidence file.** For each criterion: project name, your
   role, scope indicators
   (team size, users, revenue, cross-team reach), outcomes with metrics, and
stakeholder quotes. Aim
   for 2+ evidence items per criterion.
4. 4. 4. **Draft the packet narrative.** Executive summary (why now, in 5
   lines), then
   criterion-by-criterion evidence, then growth areas with mitigation. Keep it
scannable —
   committees read dozens.
5. 5. 5. **Line up sponsors.** Share the packet with your manager first, then
   brief 2-3 senior
   stakeholders. Ask specifically: "would you be comfortable speaking to my
scope on X in the
   calibration?" Make it easy with a one-pager.
6. 6. 6. **Pre-mortem with your manager.** Ask: "what would make this case
   fail?" Address every
   objection before the packet is submitted, not after.
7. 7. 7. **Plan the timeline.** Work backwards from the promotion cycle: packet
   draft, sponsor
   briefings, manager sign-off, submission. Build in 2-3 weeks of buffer for
revisions.

## Common pitfalls

- - - **Arguing tenure.** "I've been here 3 years" is not a criterion at any
  level. Committees promote
  on demonstrated scope, not patience.
- - - **Confusing performance with level.** Exceeding expectations at your
  current level proves you're
  great at your level — not that you're operating at the next one. The packet
must show next-level
  behaviors.
- - - **No sponsor in the room.** The best packet in the world dies if nobody
  advocates for it in
  calibration. Politics is part of the process; engage with it deliberately.
- - - **Surprising your manager.** If your manager first hears about your
  promotion ambitions when the
  packet lands, you've already lost. Align 2+ quarters ahead.
- - - **Thin evidence on influence.** Individual output is table stakes. Next
  levels require evidence
  you moved others: mentored, unblocked, aligned, decided.
- - - **Giving up after one denial.** Most promotions take 2 cycles. Treat
  denial as a detailed
  requirements doc for the next attempt — get the gaps in writing and execute.
