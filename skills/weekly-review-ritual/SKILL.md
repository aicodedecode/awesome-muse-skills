## Overview

The weekly review is the single highest-leverage hour in personal
productivity: one sitting where you clear the decks, look backward and
forward, and decide what actually matters next week. Most people skip it
because it feels formless — where do you even start? This skill gives the
ritual a shape. The assistant acts as your review partner: it walks you
through each step in conversation, holds the lists so nothing slips, asks
the questions you would forget to ask yourself, and ends the session with a
clear set of priorities instead of a vague sense of being behind. Done
weekly, it is the difference between steering your life and being towed by
it.

## When to use

- It is the end of the week (Friday afternoon or Sunday evening work best)
  and you want to close it out properly instead of carrying it into the
  weekend or Monday.
- You feel scattered — lots of open loops, no sense of what is important,
  mild dread about Monday morning.
- You want to start the week with a plan instead of reacting to whatever
  arrives first and loudest.
- Your goals or projects have drifted and you need a regular checkpoint to
  keep them alive and honest.
- You never celebrate progress and want a built-in moment to notice what
  went well — motivation needs evidence.
- You are returning from time off and need to re-orient: what did I miss,
  what matters now, what can wait.

## Core concepts

- **Get clear, get current, get creative:** the classic review arc. First
  clear the mental and digital clutter (clear), then look at the real state
  of your commitments (current), then decide what next week is for
  (creative). The order matters — you cannot prioritize with a cluttered
  head, and you cannot get creative about a future you have not looked at.
- **Open loops drain energy:** every unprocessed email, half-written note,
  and "I should really…" is an open loop your brain keeps spinning on in
  the background. The review's first job is to capture them all in one
  place so your mind can finally let go. You will feel the relief
  physically.
- **The calendar is the truth:** your intentions live in your head; your
  commitments live in your calendar. Reviewing last week (what actually
  happened vs. what you planned) and next week (what is coming, what needs
  prep) grounds the review in reality instead of aspiration.
- **Projects need a next action:** a project is not "done" or "someday" —
  it is either moving (it has a concrete next action with an owner) or
  stalled (and you should admit it). The review forces every active project
  to name its next step, which is where most planning systems quietly die.
- **Fewer priorities, honestly kept:** three priorities you actually do
  beat ten you feel guilty about. The review is where you practice saying
  no to your own list — the kindest, hardest part of the ritual.
- **Wins are data:** celebrating what went well is not vanity — it tells
  you what to repeat. People who never review wins keep optimizing for the
  wrong things and burning out on the right ones.
- **The review is a habit, not an event:** its power comes from repetition.
  A perfect review done once is worth less than a mediocre review done
  fifty weeks in a row. Design for repeatability, not impressiveness.

## Practical workflow

Block about 45–60 minutes. The assistant leads; you answer and decide. A
typical session, in order:

1. **Mind sweep (10 min).** The assistant asks: "What is on your mind —
   anything unfinished, nagging, or half-decided?" You brain-dump in
   fragments. It captures everything into one inbox list without judging or
   organizing yet. Keep going until you run dry; the last items are often
   the most important ones.
2. **Clear the inboxes (10 min).** Walk through the list together: for each
   item, decide — do it now (under 2 minutes), schedule it, delegate it,
   file it as reference, or delete it. The assistant keeps the pace brisk
   and stops you from doing real work mid-review. "That is a task, not a
   review step — captured, moving on."
3. **Review last week (5–10 min).** "What did you plan vs. what actually
   happened?" You talk through your calendar. The assistant notes what
   slipped and asks the useful question: slipped because of bad planning,
   interruption, or because it was not actually important? The answer
   changes next week's plan.
4. **Review next week (5–10 min).** Look at the coming calendar together.
   The assistant flags: anything needing prep (and schedules the prep now,
   not "later"), double-bookings or overload days, and empty space you
   should protect for deep work or rest. A week with no breathing room is
   a plan to fail — it should say so.
5. **Check goals and projects (10 min).** For each active goal or project,
   one question: "What is the next concrete action, and when?" Stalled
   projects get a decision — revive with a next action, park deliberately,
   or kill outright. The assistant remembers your goals between reviews, so
   this step gets faster and more honest each week.
6. **Choose next week's priorities (5 min).** The assistant proposes 3
   candidate priorities based on everything above; you pick, adjust, or
   replace. Write them as outcomes ("proposal draft sent"), not activities
   ("work on proposal") — outcomes are finishable, activities are not.
7. **Celebrate wins (5 min).** "What went well this week — what are you
   proud of, what worked?" Name at least three, even small ones. The
   assistant keeps a running wins log you can reread when motivation dips.
   This step is structural, not decorative.
8. **Close with a week brief.** The assistant writes one short paragraph:
   your priorities, key calendar events, and the single thing to do first
   Monday morning. Read it Monday and you are already oriented.

**The 15-minute emergency version:** when you cannot do the full hour —
mind sweep, glance at next week's calendar, pick 3 priorities. Done. A
short review beats a skipped review every time.

## Common pitfalls

- **Turning the review into work time.** The review is for deciding, not
  doing. If you start answering emails mid-review, the open loops multiply
  instead of clearing. Capture the task, keep reviewing.
- **Skipping weeks.** One missed review is a gap; two is an abandoned
  habit. If the full hour feels impossible, do the 15-minute version —
  protect the streak, not the format.
- **Reviewing without your calendar open.** Memory lies about how busy next
  week is; it edits out the dentist appointment and the school event. Always
  review with the actual calendar in front of you.
- **Carrying stale projects.** A project with no next action for three
  straight reviews is not a project — it is guilt with a name. Park it or
  kill it; both are kinder than carrying it.
- **Setting ten priorities.** The review is the moment to be honest about
  capacity. If everything is a priority, the assistant should push back:
  "Which three would make the week a win even if the rest slips?"
- **Forgetting the wins.** Ending on problems leaves you dreading the next
  review. The wins step is what makes the ritual something you look forward
  to instead of endure.
- **Letting the assistant decide for you.** It proposes, you dispose. A
  review where the assistant picks your priorities is a horoscope — the
  value is in *your* judgment, exercised weekly until it gets strong.
