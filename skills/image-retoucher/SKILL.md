## Overview

Retouching is invisible surgery: removing distractions, refining light and form,
and presenting the
subject at their best — while keeping them recognizably human. The professional
standard is simple:
nobody should be able to tell the image was retouched. This skill covers
high-end retouching
technique, from skin work to compositing cleanup.

## When to use

- Retouching portraits, beauty, or fashion photography

- Cleaning up product photos (dust, scratches, reflections)

- Compositing and background cleanup

- Restoring old or damaged photos

- Preparing images for advertising or editorial

## Core concepts

- - - **The invisibility standard.** If viewers notice the retouching, it
  failed. Every edit should
  survive the question: "does this still look like a photograph of a real
thing?"
- - - **Frequency separation.** The professional skin technique: separate
  texture (pores, fine detail)
  from tone/color (blotchiness, shadows). Fix tone on the low-frequency layer,
preserve texture on
  the high — this is what prevents plastic skin.
- - - **Dodge and burn.** Selectively lightening and darkening to sculpt form:
  enhance cheekbones,
  define jawlines, even out transitions. Subtle, built up gradually — the most
powerful and most
  abused retouching tool.
- - - **Preserve texture.** Skin has pores, fabric has weave, metal has grain.
  Smoothing that erases
  texture reads as fake instantly. When in doubt, keep more texture than feels
necessary.
- - - **Fix light, not just blemishes.** Most "bad skin" is actually bad light
  transitions. Evening
  out harsh shadow edges with dodge and burn does more than any blur filter.
- - - **Work zoomed out.** Retouch at 100% for precision, but judge at 25-50%.
  Edits that look perfect
  pixel-peeping often look overdone at viewing size. Constantly zoom out to
check.

## Practical workflow

1. 1. 1. **Assess and plan.** What's the image's purpose (beauty campaign?
   corporate headshot? product
   catalog?)? The retouching level follows the context — editorial beauty allows
more than
   documentary portraiture.
2. 2. 2. **Clean up.** Remove temporary blemishes, stray hairs, sensor dust, and
   background
   distractions (exit signs, trash, photobombers). Keep permanent features
(moles, scars) unless the
   brief says otherwise — they're identity.
3. 3. 3. **Shape with dodge and burn.** Even out skin tone transitions, sculpt
   facial structure subtly,
   clean up under-eye areas by softening (not erasing) shadows. Build gradually
at low opacity.
4. 4. 4. **Refine selectively.** Frequency separation for problematic texture
   areas only — not the
   whole face. Sharpen eyes and key details; keep everything else natural.
5. 5. 5. **Color and tone.** Final color grade, ensure skin tones are healthy
   and consistent, check for
   color casts introduced during retouching. Compare before/after at full-image
size.
6. 6. 6. **Detail check.** At 100%: halos around masked areas, mismatched grain,
   over-smoothed patches,
   warped edges from liquify. Fix or revert anything that draws the eye.
7. 7. 7. **The overnight test.** Step away; review fresh the next day at normal
   viewing distance. Fresh
   eyes catch over-retouching that tired eyes normalize.

## Common pitfalls

- - - **Plastic skin.** Blur-based smoothing that erases all texture. The #1
  retouching failure —
  learn frequency separation instead.
- - - **Over-liquifying.** Warped backgrounds, melted anatomy, impossible
  proportions. If the
  background bends, everyone notices.
- - - **Erasing identity.** Removing moles, scars, and character lines the
  subject considers
  themselves. Discuss with the subject/client what's fair game.
- - - **Inconsistent retouching across a series.** One heavily retouched hero
  and untouched supporting
  shots. Match the level across the set.
- - - **Ignoring the brief's ethics.** News, documentary, and some corporate
  contexts forbid
  manipulation beyond basic correction. Know the line for your context —
crossing it can end
  careers.
- - - **Retouching at one zoom level.** Perfect at 100%, overdone at 50%. Judge
  at the size the
  audience will see.
