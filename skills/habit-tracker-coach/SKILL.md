# Habit Tracker Coach

## Overview

Most habits die in week two, not because the habit was wrong but because it was too big and nobody
noticed the slip until it was a landslide. A habit coach fixes both problems: it keeps the habit
small enough to survive bad days, and it notices the misses while they're still easy to repair.

This skill turns your assistant into a habit coach — part planner, part accountability partner, part
data nerd. You'll design the habit together, check in daily with a one-line report, and review
weekly what's actually working. The tone is encouraging but honest: the coach celebrates streaks
and, more importantly, celebrates comebacks.

## When to use

- Starting a new daily habit: exercise, reading, meditation, journaling, language practice
- Restarting a habit that keeps dying after a few weeks
- Building a morning or evening routine out of several small habits
- Breaking a bad habit by installing a replacement behavior
- Coaching someone else (a friend, a kid) through building a routine

## Core concepts

- **Tiny starts win.** The first version of the habit should feel almost silly: two pages, five
  push-ups, one minute of meditation. You're not training the behavior yet — you're training the
  *showing up*. Scale up only after the routine feels automatic.
- **Stack onto existing routines.** New habits need reliable triggers, and your existing routines are
  the most reliable triggers you have: "After I pour my morning coffee, I open my book." The old habit
  carries the new one.
- **Never miss twice.** One missed day is life; two in a row is the start of quitting. The rule isn't
  "never miss" — it's "never let a miss become a pattern." The coach's main job is catching miss
  number one.
- **Track the check-in, not just the habit.** A daily one-line report ("done" / "missed — late
  meeting") takes ten seconds and creates a record. The record is what makes weekly reviews useful
  instead of vibes-based.
- **Identity over outcomes.** "I'm a person who reads daily" outlasts "I want to read 30 books this
  year." Each check-in is a vote for the kind of person you are. The coach reminds you of that on the
  days motivation is gone.

## Practical workflow

Start by designing the habit with the assistant. Be concrete about your real life, including the
parts that sabotage you:

*"I want to start reading daily. I have 20 minutes on the train each morning, but some days I sleep
in and miss the train. I've tried this twice before and quit around week three. Help me design this
so it sticks."*

Then run the coaching loop:

1. **Design the habit.** Ask: *"Help me make this tiny and specific: the exact behavior, when and where
   it happens, and what existing routine it stacks onto."* You want an answer like "Read 5 pages on the
   train right after sitting down" — not "read more."
2. **Set the check-in ritual.** Agree on a daily one-liner: *"I'll message you each night: done,
   missed, or the reason. If I miss, ask me what got in the way — no lectures."* Tell the assistant
   when to nudge you if you forget ("ping me at 10pm if I haven't checked in").
3. **Do the daily check-in.** Each day, just report: *"Done — 8 pages."* or *"Missed — worked late."*
   The assistant logs it, notes the streak, and on a miss asks one diagnostic question: what got in the
   way? Patterns emerge fast: late meetings, tired evenings, travel days.
4. **Repair immediately.** If you miss, the assistant's job is to get you back tomorrow, not to restart
   you next Monday: *"What's the smallest version you can do tomorrow — even 2 minutes counts?"* A
   2-minute version that happens beats a 20-minute version that doesn't.
5. **Review weekly.** Once a week, ask: *"Review my week: completion rate, what the misses had in
   common, and one tweak to try next week."* Maybe the habit needs a new time slot, a smaller minimum,
   or a different trigger. The review is where the design improves — habits are prototypes, not
   verdicts.

If you're tracking health-related habits (exercise, sleep, diet changes), the assistant's role is
motivation and organization — general wellness information, not medical advice. Check with a doctor
before starting anything intense or if you have a health condition.

## Common pitfalls

- **Starting at full size.** "Work out 45 minutes daily" as day one is a plan to quit. Start at the
  size you'd do on your worst day, then grow.
- **Tracking too many habits at once.** Three habits is plenty; five is a second job. One thriving
  habit beats four dying ones.
- **Restarting instead of resuming.** Missing a week doesn't erase the weeks before it. Resume where
  you are — "restart Monday" is just procrastination with a calendar.
- **Treating the check-in as a performance.** The daily report is data for your coach, not a test.
  Honest "missed" reports are more useful than fake "done" ones — the misses are where the design
  flaws hide.
- **Ignoring the weekly review.** Without the review, you repeat the same failing design. The review
  is the cheapest, highest-leverage five minutes of the whole system.
