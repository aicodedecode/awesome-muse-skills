# Discernment Nudge

## Overview

Discernment is the habit of pausing before believing: evaluating evidence, spotting manipulation, and updating beliefs gracefully.

In an age of viral claims and persuasive AI-generated content, discernment is a core life skill — not paranoia, but disciplined judgment.

This skill provides portable nudges: tiny checklists that trigger critical thinking exactly when it matters — before sharing, deciding, or arguing.

## When to use

- Evaluating surprising or emotional claims before sharing
- Spotting manipulation in media, marketing, or arguments
- Making decisions under hype or pressure
- Teaching critical thinking to teams or students
- Building personal resistance to misinformation

## Core concepts

- **The pause.**
  Emotional reaction (outrage, amazement, vindication) is the signal to pause, not to share. Strong emotion is manipulation's favorite vehicle.
- **Source check.**
  Who claims this? What's their track record? Do they show their work (data, methods, citations)? Anonymous viral claims start at zero credibility.
- **Evidence quality.**
  Anecdote < correlation < controlled study < meta-analysis. Ask: what would change my mind? If nothing, it's identity, not belief.
- **Incentive check.**
  Who benefits if I believe this? Follow the money, status, and tribal incentives — including your own (wanting it to be true).
- **The steelman test.**
  Can I state the opposing view fairly? If not, I don't understand the issue well enough to have a strong opinion.
- **Base rates.**
  How often are claims like this true? Extraordinary claims need extraordinary evidence — and most viral claims aren't extraordinary, just unverified.
- **Update gracefully.**
  Changing your mind with new evidence is the goal, not a failure. Track predictions; calibrate over time.
- **Nudge design.**
  Tiny friction before believing/sharing: a 3-question checklist, a 10-minute wait on outrage-bait, a 'verify before forwarding' rule.

## Practical workflow

1. **Install the pause.**
   Rule: never share anything that triggered strong emotion without a 10-minute cool-down. Set it as a phone reminder if needed.
2. **Build the 3-question checklist.**
   1) Who's the source and what's their evidence? 2) Who benefits if I believe this? 3) What would change my mind? Run it on 5 claims this week.
3. **Audit your feeds.**
   Which sources have earned trust via track record? Which trade in outrage? Prune the manipulators; the information diet shapes the mind.
4. **Practice steelmanning.**
   Weekly: take an opposing view you dislike and state its best case fairly. Discernment requires understanding, not just doubting.
5. **Track predictions.**
   Write down beliefs with confidence levels; revisit quarterly. Calibration — knowing how often you're right — is a trainable skill.
6. **Pre-commit verification.**
   Rule: verify before forwarding to groups. One quick search. The forwarder is responsible for what they spread.
7. **Teach the nudges.**
   Share the checklist with family/team. Discernment scales when it's a shared norm, not a private virtue.
8. **Review mistakes.**
   When you believed wrong: what was the tell you missed? Log it. Each error is training data for better judgment.

## Common pitfalls

- **Cynicism as discernment.**
  Doubting everything equally isn't wisdom — it's laziness. Discernment weighs evidence; cynicism dismisses it.
- **Motivated reasoning.**
  Applying scrutiny only to claims you dislike. The checklist must run on congenial claims too — especially those.
- **Sharing without verifying.**
  Forwarding because 'it might be true and important.' The forwarder owns the spread; verify first.
- **Credential worship.**
  'Expert says' without checking the expert's domain, evidence, or incentives. Credentials inform; they don't settle.
- **Anecdote overweighting.**
  One vivid story beating solid statistics. Vividness is persuasive, not probative — notice when it sways you.
- **Tribe-first truth.**
  Believing because 'our side' says it. The incentive check applies to your own tribe most of all.
- **Never updating.**
  Treating changed minds as weakness. Updating on evidence is the entire point of thinking.
- **Paralysis by analysis.**
  Demanding perfect certainty before acting. Discernment informs decisions; it doesn't replace them. Decide with calibrated confidence.
