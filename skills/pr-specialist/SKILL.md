## Overview

PR earns attention through third-party credibility: journalists, analysts, and industry voices saying you're worth noticing. This skill covers media relations (pitching, press releases, media kits), thought leadership, event/speaking strategy, and crisis communications — the full earned-media practice.

Paid, owned, earned: PR is the earned part, and its currency is newsworthiness and relationships.

## When to use

- Announcing a launch, funding round, or milestone
- Pitching journalists and publications
- Writing press releases that get picked up
- Building a thought leadership program
- Preparing for interviews or speaking engagements
- Managing a PR crisis

- Planning a thought-leadership calendar for executives
- Preparing spokespeople for broadcast interviews
- Measuring share of voice against competitors
- Managing executive visibility programs
- Handling product recall communications
- Building analyst relations
## Core concepts

**Newsworthiness.** Journalists need: timeliness, impact, novelty, conflict/tension, human interest, or data. "We launched a feature" is rarely news; "our data shows 60% of companies fail at X — here's what we found" often is. Lead with what's newsworthy, not what's important to you.

**The pitch.** Short, personalized, with the story angle upfront. Subject line like a headline. First line: why this journalist, why now. Include the news, the angle, available assets (data, customers, executives), and a clear ask (interview? coverage?). Follow up once, politely.

**Press releases.** Headline (news in one line) → subhead → dateline → lede (who/what/when/where/why in the first paragraph) → quotes (say something quotable, not corporate) → details → boilerplate → media contact. One page ideally. Embargoes and exclusives are tools — use deliberately.

**Media lists.** Quality over quantity: 20 relevant journalists beat 500 generic contacts. Track beats, recent stories, preferred contact method, and relationship history. Never blast.

**Thought leadership.** Executives as credible voices: bylined articles, data studies, conference talks, podcast appearances. The rule: teach, don't pitch. Insights that help the audience build the brand.

**Crisis communications.** Speed, honesty, empathy. Acknowledge quickly, state what you know (and don't), say what you're doing, commit to updates. One spokesperson, one source of truth. Never "no comment" — it reads as guilt.


**Message house.** Roof: the single key message. Pillars: 3 supporting messages. Foundation: proof points (data, examples) under each pillar. Every interview, release, and pitch ladders up to the house — spokespeople internalize it, journalists hear consistency.

**Newsjacking.** Inserting your perspective into breaking news cycles: monitor trending stories in your domain, respond within hours with genuine insight (not a product pitch), and offer spokespeople for comment. Speed and substance win; opportunism without insight backfires.

**Analyst relations.** Gartner, Forrester, IDC analysts influence enterprise buying enormously.
Brief analysts quarterly (not just before reports), respond to RFIs meticulously, and build genuine relationships.
Analyst placements (Magic Quadrant, Wave) move enterprise deals — invest proportionally.
**Executive visibility.** Speaking slots, bylined articles, podcast appearances, and social presence for leaders.
Executives are the most credible spokespeople — media trusts people over brands.
Build a bench (not just the CEO) and train them all.
**Measurement.** Share of voice, message pull-through, sentiment, tier-1 placements, and business impact (site traffic, inbound inquiries).
AVE (ad value equivalency) is discredited — measure outcomes, not hypothetical ad costs.
Tie PR metrics to pipeline where possible; awareness alone rarely justifies budgets.
## Practical workflow

1. **Define the story.** What's the news? Why should anyone care? Find the angle with tension or data. If there's no real news, create newsworthiness (original research, customer stories, trend commentary).
2. **Build the assets.** Press release, media kit (fact sheet, founder bios, high-res images, logos), 2–3 quotable executive quotes, customer references willing to talk, data visualizations.
3. **Target and pitch.** Research 15–30 journalists covering your beat. Personalize each pitch: reference their recent work, explain the fit. Offer exclusives to top-tier targets where it makes sense.
4. **Manage the news cycle.** Coordinate embargoes, prepare spokespeople (message house + Q&A prep), monitor coverage, amplify hits across owned channels.
5. **Build ongoing relationships.** Share useful (not self-serving) tips, comment thoughtfully on their work, be a reliable source even when you have nothing to announce. PR is a long game.
6. **Measure.** Track: coverage volume, message pull-through (did they say what you wanted?), share of voice vs. competitors, referral traffic, and downstream impact. Report monthly.

**Crisis checklist:** assemble the team → gather facts → draft holding statement → designate spokesperson → notify stakeholders → publish updates on a schedule → post-mortem and policy fixes.


**Spokesperson prep (1 hour before interviews):** review the message house → prepare 3 bridging phrases ("what's important here is...", "the bigger picture is...") → rehearse the 3 toughest questions → confirm logistics (format, duration, live vs. recorded) → agree on off-limits topics and how to deflect them.

**Crisis escalation ladder:** monitor (social listening, alerts) → assess (real issue or noise?) → activate (war room for real crises) → respond (holding statement in 1 hour, substance in 4) → resolve → learn (postmortem).
Speed with accuracy — fast wrong responses are worse than slightly slower right ones.
**Editorial calendar:** anchor on launches and milestones → layer evergreen stories → newsjack opportunistically → maintain spokespeople availability.
Proactive pitching fills 70% of coverage; reactive fills the rest.
## Common pitfalls

- **Spray-and-pray pitching.** Mass emails to irrelevant journalists burn relationships. Personalize or don't pitch.
- **No real news.** Pitching non-stories trains journalists to ignore you. Create genuine newsworthiness.
- **Corporate quotes.** "We're thrilled to announce..." says nothing. Quotes should contain the opinion or insight only a human would say.
- **Slow crisis response.** Silence fills with speculation. Respond fast, even if it's just "we're investigating and will update by 5pm."
- **"No comment."** Almost always worse than a careful partial statement. Explain what you can share and why the rest must wait.
- **Measuring clips, not impact.** Counting mentions without assessing message accuracy and business impact.
- **PR as an event.** One launch blast, then silence. Sustained relationships and story pipelines beat stunts.
- **Pitching without reading.** Contacting journalists who just wrote the opposite take. Read their recent work; reference it specifically.
- **Executive unavailability.** Big news breaks and the CEO is unreachable. Maintain a bench of trained spokespeople and a rapid-approval process.
- **Spray-and-pray pitching.** Mass emails to irrelevant journalists. Targeted, personalized pitches to 20 right journalists beat 500 generic ones.
- **No comment by default.** Declining all comment opportunities. "No comment" reads as guilt — prepare holding statements instead.
