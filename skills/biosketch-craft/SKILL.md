## Overview

The biosketch answers the reviewer's unspoken question: "Can this team
actually do this work?" This skill covers the NIH-style biosketch
(personal statement, positions, contributions to science, support),
writing contribution narratives that show trajectory rather than list
papers, and positioning yourself honestly for the proposed project.

## When to use

- Writing or updating an NIH/NSF biosketch for a proposal
- Crafting the personal statement to connect your history to this project
- Selecting which contributions and publications to highlight
- Positioning early-career investigators (and addressing thin track records)
- Reviewing a team's biosketches for gaps before submission

## Core concepts

- **The confidence question:** every element should build the case that you have the skills, track record, and resources for this specific project — generic excellence is less persuasive than specific fit.
- **Personal statement as narrative:** 1 paragraph connecting your background to the proposed work — not a CV summary, a story: "My lab developed X (refs), which positions us to do Y proposed here because Z."
- **Contributions, not publications:** the "Contributions to Science" section wants 3–5 thematic contributions with your role made explicit — what you discovered/built, why it mattered, with 3–4 key citations each.
- **Role clarity:** "led", "co-led", "developed the method", "supervised" — reviewers discount middle-author papers without stated roles; claim your actual contribution precisely.
- **Team completeness:** the set of biosketches must cover every method in the proposal — a missing expertise area is a feasibility ding; add collaborators or consultants to fill gaps deliberately.
- **Honesty constraints:** no embellishment of roles, no double-counting, complete other support (overlap disclosure) — biosketch misrepresentation is misconduct territory, not marketing.

- **The personal statement as narrative:** connect the dots — "my work on X led to Y, which positions me to do Z in this proposal" — not a list of positions; reviewers remember stories, not CVs.
- **Contributions over publications:** the contributions section should show a trajectory of solving increasingly important problems — five disconnected papers read as five disconnected ideas.
- **Synergistic activities:** service, mentoring, software, datasets, and community resources count — especially for early-career applicants, they show you're building the field, not just publishing in it.

## Practical workflow

### 1. Draft the personal statement

1. Open with your relevant expertise in one sentence; connect 2–3 past achievements to the proposed work's specific demands.
2. Name the unique assets you bring: methods you pioneered, datasets you control, collaborations you hold.
3. For early-career: emphasize training, first-author work, and the mentor/collaborator network that de-risks the project — acknowledge the stage, then neutralize it.

### 2. Write contributions as stories

1. Pick 4–5 contributions that collectively cover the proposal's methods — each with: the problem, what you did (your role explicit), the outcome/impact, 3–4 citations.
2. Order by relevance to this proposal, not chronology — the reviewer reads for fit.
3. Include a methods/tool contribution if the proposal leans on your technical innovation — "I built the instrument this project runs on" is powerful.

### 3. Assemble the supporting sections

1. **Positions and honors:** reverse chronological, complete — gaps invite questions; explain career breaks briefly and factually if needed.
2. **Publications:** selected, relevant, with your role clear — quality and relevance beat quantity; don't pad with unrelated papers.
3. **Research support:** ongoing and completed (recent 3 years typically) — show it's related but not overlapping; disclose overlaps explicitly with explanations of distinct aims.

### 4. Review as a team

1. Lay all key-personnel biosketches side by side: does every proposed method have a named expert? Fill gaps with collaborators now, not after review.
2. Check consistency: effort levels match the budget; roles match the narrative's description of who does what.
3. Update or perish: a biosketch more than a year old signals staleness — refresh contributions and support every submission.

### 5. Tailor the biosketch to the proposal

1. Reorder contributions to lead with the work most relevant to this proposal — relevance beats chronology.
2. In the personal statement, name the specific expertise each aim requires and map it to your (or the team's) track record — leave no aim's feasibility to implication.
3. For team proposals, ensure complementary — not overlapping — expertise across biosketches; redundancy wastes space that could cover gaps.

### 6. Quick-reference checklist

- [ ] Personal statement tells the story: X → Y → positions me for Z
- [ ] Contributions ordered by relevance to this proposal
- [ ] Track record mapped explicitly to each aim's required expertise
- [ ] Statement tailored to this proposal (not a generic swap)
- [ ] Synergistic activities show field-building, not just publishing
- [ ] Complementary (not overlapping) expertise across team biosketches
- [ ] Roles clarified on large consortium papers
- [ ] Current format requirements of the funder followed

## Common pitfalls

- **Generic personal statements:** "Dr. X is an expert in Y with 50 publications" — says nothing about fit to this project; rewrite as a targeted narrative.
- **Contribution vagueness:** listing papers without stating what you contributed — reviewers can't credit what they can't see.
- **Padding:** unrelated publications, honorary authorships presented as leadership — dilutes the real signal.
- **Missing expertise:** proposing single-cell/spatial/AI methods with no team member who's done them — the #1 feasibility killer.
- **Stale biosketches:** 3-year-old support sections, missing recent key papers — suggests the application was assembled carelessly.
- **Overlap blindness:** undisclosed scientific/budgetary overlap across grants — disclose and differentiate proactively.
- **The generic personal statement:** one statement reused across five proposals with the title swapped — reviewers can tell; tailor it.
- **Padding with minor roles:** listing large consortium papers where your role was peripheral without clarifying — it dilutes the real contributions; be honest about roles.
