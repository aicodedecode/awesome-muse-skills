## Overview

A brand identity is the complete system through which a company presents itself: strategy (who we
are, for whom, why), visual language (logo, color, typography, imagery), voice (how we sound), and
the rules that keep it all coherent. This skill builds identities from strategy down to deliverables
— because visuals without strategy are decoration, and strategy without visuals is a memo.

## When to use

- Creating a brand identity for a new company or product

- Rebranding an existing business

- Building brand guidelines from scratch

- Auditing an inconsistent brand across touchpoints

- Extending an identity to new products or markets

## Core concepts

- - **Strategy before visuals.** Positioning (what space do we own?), audience (who exactly?),
  personality (how would the brand act at a dinner party?), and differentiators. Every visual choice
  should trace back to strategy.
- - **The identity system, not just a logo.** Logo, color palette, typography scale, imagery style,
  iconography, patterns/textures, motion principles. The system matters more than any single element
  — it's what makes a brand recognizable with the logo removed.
- - **Voice and tone.** How the brand writes: 3-4 voice attributes (e.g., "confident, warm,
  plain-spoken") with do/don't examples. Voice is half the identity and the most neglected half.
- - **Flexibility within consistency.** A rigid identity breaks on new formats; a loose one
  dissolves. Define the non-negotiables (logo clearspace, core colors) and the playground
  (illustration styles, campaign treatments).
- - **Touchpoint audit.** List every place the brand appears — website, app, packaging, social,
  email, office, invoices. The identity must work across all of them, and the audit reveals which
  need the most help.
- - **Guidelines people actually use.** A 200-page brand book nobody opens is worse than a 20-page
  one everyone follows. Prioritize: logo rules, color, typography, voice examples, and common
  templates.

## Practical workflow

1. 1. **Discovery and strategy.** Stakeholder interviews, audience research, competitor audit.
   Output: positioning statement, personality attributes, and 3-5 brand pillars. Get sign-off before
   any visual work.
2. 2. **Moodboards and directions.** 2-3 distinct visual directions (not logos yet — overall feel:
   color, type, imagery). Present with strategy rationale; let stakeholders react to feelings before
   forms.
3. 3. **Design the core system.** Logo (see logo-designer), color palette (primary, secondary,
   neutrals with exact values), typography (display + text faces with scale), imagery direction
   (photography style or illustration style).
4. 4. **Build the supporting elements.** Iconography style, patterns or graphic devices, layout
   principles, motion/feel guidelines for digital. Test the system: can you design 5 different
   touchpoints that clearly belong together?
5. 5. **Define voice.** Voice attributes with examples, tone adjustments by context (marketing vs
   support vs legal), and a word list (we say X, not Y).
6. 6. **Apply to real touchpoints.** Design 5-8 key applications: homepage hero, social profile,
   business card or email signature, packaging or app icon, one campaign piece. Real applications
   prove the system works.
7. 7. **Write the guidelines.** Concise, visual, practical: the rules, the files, the templates, and
   who to ask. Include don'ts with visual examples — they prevent more damage than the do's create
   value.

## Common pitfalls

- - **Logo-first branding.** Designing the mark before strategy produces a pretty picture with no
  foundation. The logo is maybe 10% of an identity.
- - **Strategy nobody believes.** Positioning written to please the CEO rather than reflect reality.
  If employees roll their eyes, the identity is fiction.
- - **Inconsistent application.** The identity dies in a thousand small cuts: off-brand decks, rogue
  social posts, outdated templates. Governance (who approves what) matters as much as design.
- - **Designing for the launch, not the decade.** Trendy identities age fast. Test: will this still
  feel right in 5 years, across formats that don't exist yet?
- - **Ignoring internal brand.** Employees are the primary audience. If the team doesn't understand
  and embody the identity, no guideline document will save it.
- - **The guideline graveyard.** Over-detailed manuals that nobody reads. Keep it short, visual, and
  focused on the decisions people actually face weekly.
