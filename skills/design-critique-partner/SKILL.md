## Overview

Critique is design's quality control — but most "feedback" is either vague
praise or personal taste
masquerading as judgment. A good critique partner evaluates work against its
goals, separates
problems from preferences, and delivers feedback the designer can actually act
on. This skill
provides critique frameworks for both giving and receiving design feedback.

## When to use

- Reviewing a design before stakeholder presentation or launch

- Running team critique sessions (design crits)

- Giving feedback on a colleague's or freelancer's work

- Preparing to receive critique on your own designs

- Building a culture of honest feedback

## Core concepts

- - - **Critique against goals, not taste.** Every critique starts with: what is
  this trying to
  achieve, for whom? "I don't like blue" is taste; "the blue CTA doesn't stand
out against the blue
  hero, hurting conversion" is critique.
- - - **The 3-level framework.** Level 1: Does it work? (goals, usability,
  clarity). Level 2: Is it
  coherent? (hierarchy, consistency, craft). Level 3: Is it excellent?
(distinctiveness, delight,
  memorability). Fix level 1 before debating level 3.
- - - **Specific, observable, actionable.** "The headline hierarchy is unclear
  because the subhead is
  larger than the headline on mobile" beats "the typography feels off." Point to
the thing, explain
  the effect, suggest a direction.
- - - **Questions over declarations.** "What happens when the username is 40
  characters?" or "What's
  the primary action you want here?" Questions invite thinking; declarations
invite defensiveness.
- - - **Separate the problem from the solution.** "Users might miss the CTA" is
  the problem
  (valuable). "Make it red and bigger" is a solution (maybe wrong). Good
critique identifies
  problems clearly and lets the designer solve.
- - - **Receiving critique well.** Listen without defending, ask clarifying
  questions, separate useful
  signal from noise, and decide later what to change. Defensiveness in the room
kills future
  honesty.

## Practical workflow

1. 1. 1. **Set up the critique.** Presenter shares: the goal, the audience,
   constraints, and what
   feedback they need (what's decided vs open). Timebox: 20-30 minutes per
piece.
2. 2. 2. **Silent review first.** 3-5 minutes of silent looking. Prevents
   anchoring on the first loud
   opinion and gives introverts equal footing.
3. 3. 3. **Clarifying round.** Questions only — no opinions yet. Ensures
   everyone understands what
   they're looking at before judging it.
4. 4. 4. **Structured feedback.** Go level by level: does it achieve the goal?
   (biggest issues first),
   then craft and coherence, then polish. Each person: one strength, then
concerns with specifics.
5. 5. 5. **Prioritize the fixes.** End with: the 2-3 changes that matter most,
   ranked. A list of 20
   nits helps nobody; the top 3 change the design.
6. 6. 6. **Document decisions.** What feedback was accepted, what was rejected
   (with reason), and
   what's still open. Prevents re-litigating the same points next crit.
7. 7. 7. **Follow up.** Review the revision against the critique. Close the loop
   — critique without
   follow-through is just opinion theater.

## Common pitfalls

- - - **Taste disguised as critique.** "I would have done it differently"
  without tying to goals.
  Always anchor feedback to the stated objectives.
- - - **Praise sandwiches.** Burying real feedback in fluff. Professionals can
  handle direct, kind,
  specific critique — the sandwich just confuses the message.
- - - **Designing in the crit.** The group art-directing pixel by pixel.
  Critique identifies problems;
  the designer owns solutions. Stop the group redesign.
- - - **Piling on.** Five people repeating the same point. Facilitator's job:
  acknowledge, capture
  once, move on.
- - - **Critiquing the person.** "You always..." or questioning competence.
  Critique the work, in this
  version, against these goals. Nothing else.
- - - **No goals stated.** Critiquing without knowing what the design is for.
  The first question is
  always: "what's this trying to do?" — if nobody knows, that's the feedback.
