# Habit Tracker

## Overview

Habits are behaviors on autopilot — the closest thing to free productivity.

Formation loop: cue -> routine -> reward. Tracking adds feedback: the checkmark becomes part of the reward.

Tracking alone doesn't build habits. The habit must be tiny, cue-linked, and rewarding enough to repeat.

Effective systems: 3-7 habits max, seconds-per-day tracking, misses treated as data not failure.

Design for your lowest-energy self. Motivation starts habits; systems sustain them.

## When to use

- Starting new habits (exercise, reading, meditation, writing, languages)
- Breaking bad habits by replacing them with better ones
- Choosing a habit-tracking app or analog method
- Recovering after falling off a routine
- Designing team or family habit challenges

## Core concepts

- **Start ridiculously small.**
  'Read 2 pages' sticks; 'read 20 pages' fails. Initial goal is showing up, not output. Scale after the identity forms.
- **Habit stacking.**
  Attach to an existing habit: 'After coffee, I journal.' Existing habits are the most reliable cues available.
- **Implementation intentions.**
  'I will [behavior] at [time] in [location].' Specificity beats motivation. 'Walk 15 min at 7am' works; 'exercise more' doesn't.
- **Environment design.**
  Good habits obvious (shoes by door), bad habits hard (phone in another room). Willpower is unreliable; friction is forever.
- **Never miss twice.**
  One miss is an accident; two is a new habit of skipping. After a slip, resume immediately — never 'restart Monday.'
- **Identity over outcomes.**
  'I'm a reader' outlasts 'lose 5kg.' Each completion is a vote for the identity. Track the votes.
- **Track, don't judge.**
  The tracker is a dashboard, not a courtroom. Gaps reveal obstacles to design around, not failures to feel bad about.
- **Minimum viable version.**
  Define the bad-day version of each habit explicitly. The 2-minute version keeps the streak of showing up alive.

## Practical workflow

1. **Choose 3-5 habits max.**
   Fewer than you want. Each under 10 minutes initially, with a 2-minute starter version for bad days.
2. **Write implementation intentions.**
   Exact behavior, cue, time, place — plus the minimum version. Vague plans produce vague results.
3. **Pick a tracking method.**
   App, spreadsheet, wall calendar, notebook grid. Under 10 seconds per habit per day, visible, satisfying to mark.
4. **Design the environment first.**
   Change one thing per habit before day one: reduce friction or add a cue. Don't wait to fail first.
5. **Set a weekly review.**
   Which habits are automatic? Which need redesign (size, cue, time) rather than more willpower? Adjust the system.
6. **Handle slips by protocol.**
   Miss once: note cause, resume tomorrow. Miss twice: shrink the habit or change the cue immediately.
7. **Evolve quarterly.**
   Graduate easy habits, retire automatic ones from active tracking, add one new habit at a time. Keep the list short forever.
8. **Pair habits with rewards.**
   After the routine, something genuinely pleasant — even just marking the X. The loop needs its reward to close.

## Common pitfalls

- **Too many habits at once.**
  Ten new habits in January, zero by February. One at a time is faster long-term because it survives.
- **All-or-nothing tracking.**
  Binary pass/fail punishes the 20-minute walk that was still worthwhile. Partial credit keeps showing up alive.
- **Tracking without design.**
  Logging failures for months without changing cue, size, or environment. Data without redesign is a guilt log.
- **Streak obsession.**
  A broken 100-day streak can end the habit ('it's ruined'). Value the 90%-of-days trend over the unbroken chain.
- **Vague habits.**
  'Be healthier' can't be tracked. 'No soda on weekdays' can. No checkmark possible = not a habit yet.
- **Relying on motivation.**
  Design every habit for your busiest, most distracted, lowest-energy self — that's who has to do it.
- **Punishing rest.**
  7-days-a-week with no recovery leads to burnout and abandonment. Schedule rest days deliberately.
- **Copying someone's stack.**
  Borrow principles, not schedules. Build from your life, chronotype, and constraints — not an influencer's routine.
