# Meal Plan Generator

## Overview

Weekly meal planning fails when it's treated as a rigid contract: seven days of recipes you'll
abandon by Wednesday. Good meal planning is looser — it's a system that answers two questions:
"what's for dinner tonight?" and "what do I buy on Saturday?" without daily stress.

This skill helps you build a meal plan conversationally with your assistant. You'll state your
preferences, constraints, and cooking reality; the assistant drafts a week of meals, a grocery list,
and a batch-cooking strategy. The emphasis is on flexibility: the plan bends around your week
instead of breaking on contact with it. Think of it as a framework with options, not a timetable.

## When to use

- Setting up a weekly cooking routine that reduces takeout and decision fatigue
- Planning meals around dietary needs (vegetarian, allergies, low-sugar, high-protein)
- Feeding a household with mixed preferences without cooking three separate dinners
- Cutting the grocery bill by buying with a plan instead of impulse shopping
- Cooking for one and wanting to avoid food waste

## Core concepts

- **Constraints first, recipes second.** Your plan starts from your real life: how many people, what
  you don't eat, how long you'll cook on a weeknight, your budget. Recipes that ignore these are
  decoration.
- **Plan around anchors, not all 21 meals.** Most people only need dinner planned, plus a rotating
  breakfast and simple lunches. Planning every single meal is overkill and breeds resentment.
- **Leftovers are an ingredient, not a failure.** Design meals that deliberately produce components —
  a big pot of rice, roasted vegetables, cooked lentils — that become tomorrow's lunch or the base of
  another dinner.
- **Batch the boring parts.** Washing and chopping vegetables, cooking grains, making one sauce — done
  once on the weekend, these shave 20 minutes off every weeknight meal.
- **Flexibility is the feature.** Build in swap slots: "any stir-fry," "clean-out-the-fridge soup,"
  one night explicitly reserved for takeout or leftovers. A plan with built-in slack survives; a plan
  without it doesn't.

## Practical workflow

Start with a brief that covers your real constraints. Be honest — the plan only works if the
assistant knows your actual life:

*"Plan my week of dinners for 2 people. We're vegetarian, my partner dislikes mushrooms, and I won't
cook for more than 30 minutes on weeknights. Budget about ₹2,500 for groceries this week. We have a
pressure cooker and a microwave."*

Then build it up in layers:

1. **Draft the week.** *"Suggest 7 dinners using these constraints. Include one batch-cook night, one
   leftovers night, and one easy/optional takeout night. Keep the cuisine varied."* Review the draft
   and veto what you'll never actually cook — honesty here saves the whole week.
2. **Get the grocery list.** *"Turn this into a grocery list organized by section: produce, dairy,
   grains, spices. Mark what I probably already have with a question mark so I can check."* Ask it to
   flag anything used by only one recipe — those are the waste risks.
3. **Plan the prep session.** *"What can I batch-cook on Sunday in under 90 minutes to make weeknights
   faster?"* One focused prep session is worth more than five ambitious recipes.
4. **Design the leftovers flow.** *"For each dinner, tell me what the leftovers become — next day's
   lunch or part of another meal."* Cook once, eat twice is the whole economics of meal planning.
5. **Keep a running list.** During the week, when something runs out or a recipe flops, tell the
   assistant: *"We're out of rice and the Tuesday curry was too bland — adjust next week's plan."* The
   plan gets smarter every week if you feed it feedback.

Nutrition notes the assistant gives you are general educational information, not medical or dietary
advice. If you have a health condition, allergies beyond the ordinary, or specific nutritional
targets, check the plan with a doctor or registered dietitian.

## Common pitfalls

- **Cooking seven new recipes.** New recipes take twice as long and produce twice the dishes. Aim for
  2–3 new ones max; the rest should be familiar or assembly-style meals.
- **Buying specialty ingredients for one dish.** That one jar of an obscure paste used once becomes
  pantry clutter. Prefer recipes that share ingredients with each other.
- **Skipping the swap slots.** If every night is fixed, the first busy evening kills the plan. Protect
  the leftovers night and the easy night — they're load-bearing.
- **Planning portions for a fantasy appetite.** Cook for the people you actually feed, and be
  realistic about how much you'll eat. "Serves 4" recipes for 2 people are a feature (lunch tomorrow),
  not a bug — as long as you planned for it.
- **Not reviewing what worked.** Spend two minutes on Sunday asking: what got eaten, what got wasted,
  what took too long? Next week's plan starts there.
