## Overview

Memorable characters are designed, not drawn — behind every iconic character is
deliberate work on
silhouette, shape language, personality, and consistency. This skill covers
character design from
concept and reference through silhouette exploration, expression, posing, and
the production sheets
(turnarounds, expression sheets) that let others draw the character
consistently.

## When to use

- Designing original characters for games, animation, or comics

- Creating mascots or brand characters

- Developing character lineups and casts

- Building expression sheets and turnarounds

- Reviewing character designs for appeal and readability

## Core concepts

- - - **Silhouette is king.** Fill your character with black — is it instantly
  recognizable?
  Distinctive silhouettes (think Mickey's ears, Sonic's spikes) are what make
characters iconic and
  readable at any size.
- - - **Shape language.** Circles = friendly/soft, squares = sturdy/reliable,
  triangles =
  dynamic/dangerous. Consistent shape language across a character's design
communicates personality
  before any detail does.
- - - **Personality drives design.** Who is this character? Their history,
  desires, and flaws should
  show in posture, clothing, and expression. Design decisions need character
reasons, not just
  aesthetic ones.
- - - **Appeal over realism.** Appeal (the pleasing, interesting quality)
  matters more than anatomical
  correctness in stylized work. Exaggerate what matters, simplify what doesn't.
- - - **The lineup test.** Characters in a cast must be distinguishable by
  silhouette, color palette,
  and proportions at a glance. If two characters blur together, redesign one.
- - - **Consistency systems.** Turnarounds (front/side/back/three-quarter),
  expression sheets, pose
  guides, color palettes with exact values, and construction notes (how are the
hands built? what's
  the head-to-body ratio?). Others can only draw what you've documented.

## Practical workflow

1. 1. 1. **Write the character brief.** Who are they — personality, role in the
   story, age, world?
   Gather visual references: real-world inspiration, existing characters in
adjacent space (to
   differentiate from), costume/prop research.
2. 2. 2. **Explore silhouettes.** 20-30 black silhouettes exploring different
   proportions, shapes, and
   reads. Pick the 3-5 with the strongest, most distinctive silhouettes —
details come later.
3. 3. 3. **Develop the winners.** Add internal detail to the best silhouettes:
   face, costume, key
   props. Keep exploring variations — swap heads, change costumes, push
proportions.
4. 4. 4. **Test personality.** Draw the character in 3-5 expressions and 2-3
   poses that reflect their
   personality. A character that only works in a neutral standing pose isn't
designed yet.
5. 5. 5. **Refine the final.** Clean up the chosen design: consistent line
   weight, finalized colors
   (limited palette — 3-5 colors), simplified details that survive at small
sizes.
6. 6. 6. **Build the model sheets.** Turnaround (front, side, back, 3/4),
   expression sheet (6-12
   expressions), pose sheet, color palette with values, prop details, and
construction/do-don't
   notes.
7. 7. 7. **Stress-test.** Draw the character from imagination in a new pose.
   Hand the sheet to someone
   else — can they draw the character recognizably? If not, the documentation
needs work.

## Common pitfalls

- - - **Same-face syndrome.** Every character sharing one face with different
  hair. Push facial
  structure, not just hairstyles — faces carry identity.
- - - **Over-detailing.** Intricate designs that can't be drawn consistently or
  read at small sizes.
  Simplify: if a detail doesn't serve silhouette or personality, cut it.
- - - **No personality in the design.** Technically skilled drawing of a generic
  person. The brief's
  personality work isn't optional — it's the design's foundation.
- - - **Inconsistent proportions.** The character looks different in every
  drawing. Lock the
  construction (head units, key measurements) and check against the turnaround.
- - - **Designing in a vacuum.** No consideration of the cast, world, or medium
  (a game character
  needs readability at gameplay distance; a comic character needs expressive
range).
- - - **Skipping the turnaround.** "I'll figure out the back view later"
  produces characters that
  change between panels. Model sheets aren't bureaucracy — they're the design's
memory.
