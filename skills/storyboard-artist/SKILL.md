## Overview

Storyboards are filmmaking's first draft: they translate scripts into shots,
revealing pacing
problems, unclear geography, and weak visual storytelling before a single frame
is shot. A good
storyboard artist thinks like a director — camera language, staging, and cutting
— and draws just
clearly enough to communicate, never more. Speed and clarity beat polish.

## When to use

- Storyboarding films, commercials, or animation

- Pre-visualizing product videos or explainers

- Planning shot sequences for client approval

- Communicating directorial vision to a crew

- Learning visual storytelling and film grammar

## Core concepts

- - - **Film grammar.** Shot sizes (extreme wide → close-up) control intimacy
  and information; angles
  (low = power, high = vulnerability) control psychology; movement (pan, dolly,
handheld) controls
  energy. Every shot choice should be intentional.
- - - **The 180-degree rule.** Keep the camera on one side of the action axis or
  the audience loses
  spatial orientation. Break it only deliberately (disorientation as effect).
- - **Staging and composition.** Rule of thirds, leading lines, depth layers
  (foreground/midground/background), and eye-lines. Each panel should guide the
eye to the story
  point instantly.
- - - **Cutting on action and emotion.** Storyboards show not just shots but why
  we cut: to follow
  action, to reveal reaction, to compress time. Note the motivation for key
cuts.
- - - **Clarity over beauty.** Storyboard panels are communication tools, not
  gallery pieces. Clear
  staging, readable expressions, and accurate geography beat rendered shading
every time. Directors
  storyboard in stick figures when needed.
- - - **Pacing on the page.** Panel density and size suggest timing: big panels
  linger, sequences of
  small panels accelerate. Animatics (timed slideshows) test pacing before
production.

## Practical workflow

1. 1. 1. **Break down the script.** Identify beats: what changes in each moment?
   Mark the emotional
   turns — those get the close-ups. List locations, characters, and key actions
per scene.
2. 2. 2. **Choose coverage.** For each beat: the master shot (geography), then
   coverage (close-ups,
   inserts, reactions). Plan the minimum shots that tell the story —
over-coverage wastes shoot
   days.
3. 3. 3. **Thumbnail the sequence.** Rough panels, fast: one thumbnail per shot,
   focusing on
   composition and camera. Explore 2-3 framings for key moments.
4. 4. 4. **Refine key panels.** Clean up staging, add arrows for camera/subject
   movement, note shot
   size and lens feel, add dialogue/action captions. Every panel answers: what
do we see, what
   moves, what do we hear?
5. 5. 5. **Check the flow.** Read the board as a film: does geography stay
   clear? Does the cutting
   build? Are there jump cuts or confusing transitions? Fix on paper — it's free
here.
6. 6. 6. **Add technical notes.** Camera movement, transitions
   (cut/dissolve/whip), VFX notes, timing
   estimates. The board becomes the shoot's shared reference.
7. 7. 7. **Present and revise.** Walk stakeholders through the board as the
   film: narrate it. Note what
   confuses them — that's what would confuse the audience. Revise before
production locks.

## Common pitfalls

- - - **Over-rendering panels.** Spending hours shading a panel that gets
  redrawn after the first
  script revision. Communicate clearly, render later (if ever).
- - - **Unclear geography.** Audience can't tell where characters are relative
  to each other.
  Establish with a wide, maintain with the 180 rule, re-establish after complex
movement.
- - - **Every shot a close-up (or wide).** No variety in shot sizes flattens the
  visual rhythm. Vary
  deliberately: wide for context, close for emotion.
- - - **Ignoring the cut.** Drawing beautiful panels with no thought to how they
  connect. Filmmaking
  is the cut — plan transitions, not just frames.
- - - **Too many/few panels.** Over-boarding every micro-beat wastes time;
  under-boarding leaves the
  crew guessing. Board the story beats and the tricky technical shots.
- - - **No movement notation.** Static panels with no arrows or notes leave
  camera and subject
  movement ambiguous — the most common storyboard communication failure.
