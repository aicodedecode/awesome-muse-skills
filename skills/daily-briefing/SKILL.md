# Daily Briefing

## Overview

A daily briefing is a short morning conversation with Muse that sets the tone for the day. Instead of discovering your schedule one surprise at a time, you get a single concise summary: what happened overnight, what's on your calendar, what the weather means for your plans, and — most importantly — the top three things that actually matter today. The whole ritual takes five minutes and replaces the anxious first hour of reactive checking. It's a conversation, not an app: you talk, Muse assembles and prioritizes, you adjust.

## When to use

- Every morning, as the start-of-day ritual (5 minutes, same time each day works best)
- When your calendar is packed and you need help deciding what deserves your energy
- Before travel days, to check weather, meetings, and timing in one pass
- When you wake up to unread messages and need help separating urgent from noise
- When you want to start the day intentionally instead of reactively

## Core concepts

- **One conversation, not five tabs.**
  The value is consolidation: priorities, calendar, weather, and messages in a single chat summary instead of scattered across apps.
- **Top 3 focus items.**
  Everything else is context; three items are commitments. Three is small enough to remember and big enough to matter. Muse proposes them; you approve or swap.
- **Weather-aware planning.**
  Weather changes clothing, commute timing, and whether outdoor plans are sensible. A briefing that ignores weather is just a calendar readout.
- **Overnight triage.**
  Some messages that arrived overnight are time-sensitive; most aren't. The briefing filters so you only act on the genuinely urgent before noon.
- **Calendar is truth, not suggestion.**
  The briefing reads your calendar as it is. If something's wrong there, fix the calendar first — briefing quality depends on calendar quality.
- **Evenings feed mornings.**
  A thirty-second evening note ("tomorrow's big thing is the client call") makes the next morning's briefing sharper. The briefing is a loop, not a broadcast.
- **Prep items get named, not implied.**
  If something needs preparation, the briefing should say what and by when — "prep 20 min for the 2pm review" — not just list the event.

## Practical workflow

1. **Say good morning with context.**
   Open with something like: "Good morning — give me my briefing for today." If it's your first time, add the basics: your timezone, your rough hours, and what "a good day" looks like for you this week.
2. **Share what Muse can see.**
   Tell Muse where to look: "Check my calendar for today and any messages from overnight." Name the sources you care about — calendar, email, chat — so it doesn't guess.
3. **Ask for weather that affects plans.**
   Prompt: "How's the weather today, and does it affect anything on my calendar?" Muse connects conditions to your actual plans — commute, outdoor meetings, errands.
4. **Get the top 3 proposed.**
   Ask: "What's my top 3 focus for today?" Muse weighs deadlines, calendar density, and what you told it matters this week, then proposes three. Push back: "Swap item 2 — that can wait; instead..." The negotiation is the point.
5. **Flag the landmines.**
   Ask: "Anything tonight or tomorrow that changes what I do today?" Prep work, early meetings, and deadlines have a way of hiding. Surface them now.
6. **Confirm and close.**
   End with: "Summarize my day in three lines so I can glance back later." You get a compact reference you can re-open any time. Then close the laptop for a minute and breathe — the briefing's job is done.

## Common pitfalls

- **Letting the briefing become a to-do dump.**
  Fifteen items is not a briefing, it's anxiety. Insist on the top 3; everything else is just listed, not committed to.
- **Skipping the weather question.**
  It's the easiest thing to forget and the most common cause of a ruined plan. Make it a fixed part of the ritual.
- **Briefing without an accurate calendar.**
  If your calendar is missing half your commitments, the briefing optimizes the wrong day. Keep the calendar honest and the briefing stays useful.
- **Treating the top 3 as rigid.**
  Life happens. The top 3 is a compass, not a contract — rearrange when reality changes, and tell Muse so it learns your actual priorities.
- **Doing the briefing too late.**
  A briefing at 10:30 after you've already reacted to everything is a debrief. Same time each morning, before the noise starts.
- **Never customizing the format.**
  The default summary works for week one. By week two, tell Muse what to keep and cut: "Skip the news section; always include my workout." The ritual should fit you.
- **Skipping weekends and travel days.**
  The days you need a briefing most — travel, Mondays after a holiday — are the easiest to skip. Those are the days that pay for the habit.
- **Trigger phrases for Muse.**
  Phrases like "start my day," "morning briefing," or "what's my day look like?" should always trigger this ritual once you've set it up. Tell Muse that explicitly so it doesn't need re-explaining.
- **Briefing a partner or team.**
  Once your own ritual works, extend it: "Give me a two-line summary I can share with my partner about today's logistics." Shared mornings run smoother.
- **Briefing on the go.**
  Mornings happen in cars and on trains too. The briefing is just a chat — voice or text both work. The format survives either medium.
- **Turning the briefing into planning.**
  Briefing is orientation; planning is separate work. If the summary keeps growing into a planning session, split them — five minutes to orient, then plan separately if needed.
- **Ignoring energy, not just time.**
  A packed calendar of low-energy admin after a late night is a different day than the same calendar when rested. Tell Muse your energy; let it sequence accordingly.
