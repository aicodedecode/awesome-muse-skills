## Overview

A literature review is not a bibliography with verbs: it synthesizes
what's known, adjudicates disagreements, and exposes the gaps your work
fills. This skill covers systematic searching, organizing by theme
rather than by paper, writing critical synthesis (comparing methods and
findings, not listing them), and identifying genuine gaps versus
crowded territory.

## When to use

- Writing a standalone review article or a thesis literature chapter
- Surveying a field before starting a project (and documenting it)
- Identifying where the real gaps are — and where the field only thinks there are gaps
- Synthesizing conflicting findings into a coherent account
- Deciding when to stop reading and start writing

## Core concepts

- **Synthesis vs summary:** summary says "A found X; B found Y"; synthesis says "A and B disagree on X because their methods differ in Z; the weight of evidence favors..." — the review's value is entirely in the second move.
- **Thematic organization:** group by question, mechanism, or method — never by author ("Smith (2020) found... Jones (2021) found...") — the annotated-bibliography structure is the classic failed review.
- **Critical appraisal:** for each claim, note the evidence quality — sample size, design, controls, replication status. A review that treats all papers as equal misleads.
- **Search as method:** databases, keywords, inclusion/exclusion criteria, snowballing from key papers, grey literature awareness — document the strategy so the review is auditable (PRISMA-style even for narrative reviews).
- **Gap taxonomy:** unstudied questions, conflicting evidence, methodological limitations, untested boundary conditions, unintegrated findings — different gaps demand different follow-up work; name the type.
- **Saturation:** you stop when new papers stop changing the synthesis, not when you've read everything — diminishing returns are real; set a cutoff date and state it.

- **Citation context:** don't just cite — characterize: does this paper support, contradict, or extend the claim? Citation without context is name-dropping.
- **Weighting evidence:** not all papers are equal — sample size, design quality, replication status, and retraction checks determine how much weight a finding gets in your synthesis.
- **The "review of reviews" trap:** citing other reviews instead of primary sources propagates their errors and interpretations — go to the primary literature for load-bearing claims.

## Practical workflow

### 1. Search systematically

1. Define the question and scope narrowly — "reviews of everything about X" become unreadable; "how does X affect Y under Z conditions" stays tractable.
2. Build keyword sets (synonyms, MeSH terms, field jargon variants); search 2+ databases; snowball citations forward and backward from 3–5 seed papers.
3. Screen by title/abstract with explicit inclusion/exclusion criteria; keep a screening log — future-you (and reviewers) will ask what was excluded and why.

### 2. Extract and organize

1. Build an extraction table: paper → question, method, key finding, n/design quality, limitations — one row per paper, sortable by theme.
2. Cluster into 3–6 themes (questions, mechanisms, methods, populations); each theme becomes a section.
3. Within each theme, arrange findings to build an argument: establish → complicate → resolve-or-gap.

### 3. Write synthesis, not lists

1. Open each section with the theme's central question and the current state of play — in your words.
2. Compare and adjudicate: "Three studies report X using [method]; two report Y using [different method]. The discrepancy likely reflects [Z], because..." — cite the evidence for your adjudication.
3. Use tables/figures for what prose handles badly: method comparisons, effect-size summaries, timelines of the field's development.

### 4. Close with gaps and agenda

1. State the gaps precisely, typed by the taxonomy above — "more research is needed" is not a gap statement.
2. Propose what would resolve each: the experiment, dataset, or method that would settle it.
3. End with the field's trajectory: where the converging evidence points, and what the next five years should do.

### 5. Build the synthesis table

1. Create a structured table: study → design/n → key finding → effect size → limitations — the review's analytical backbone, often publishable as a supplementary table.
2. Sort and group by the themes of your narrative, not chronologically — the table serves the argument.
3. Use the table to spot the gaps mechanically: empty cells (untested combinations), conflicting rows (disagreements needing adjudication), weak-design clusters (findings needing replication).

### 6. Quick-reference checklist

- [ ] Every claim cited with characterized context (support/contradict/extend)
- [ ] Primary sources used for load-bearing claims, not reviews-of-reviews
- [ ] Retraction status checked for key citations
- [ ] Evidence weighted by design quality, not vote-counted
- [ ] Synthesis organized by themes, not author-by-author
- [ ] Gap/controversy stated explicitly and justified by the synthesis
- [ ] Search strategy documented (for systematic reviews: PRISMA)
- [ ] Summary table of studies prepared (analytical backbone)

## Common pitfalls

- **Annotated bibliography structure:** paper-by-paper paragraphs with no synthesis — restructure by theme.
- **Uncritical inclusion:** citing retracted, underpowered, or methodologically broken work alongside solid studies without distinction.
- **Recency bias:** overweighting the last two years — foundational work earns its place by being foundational; check citation context, not just dates.
- **Strawmanning to create a gap:** misrepresenting prior work to manufacture novelty — reviewers in the field will notice and punish.
- **Scope creep:** the review keeps expanding until it's unfinishable — freeze the scope and cutoff date early.
- **No stopping rule:** reading forever instead of writing — saturation + cutoff date beats completeness.
- **Vote-counting:** "5 studies found X, 3 found Y, so X wins" — ignores sample sizes, quality, and effect sizes; synthesize quantitatively (meta-analysis) or qualitatively with weighting, never by counting.
- **Citing retracted work:** failing to check retraction status of key citations — a retracted pillar collapses the synthesis built on it.
