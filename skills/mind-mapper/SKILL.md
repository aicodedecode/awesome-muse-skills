# Mind Mapper

## Overview

Mind maps externalize thinking spatially: a central idea with branching associations, keywords, colors, and images.

They suit the brain's associative nature — great for brainstorming, planning, studying, and seeing connections linear notes hide.

Effective maps: one central image, one keyword per branch, hierarchy by proximity, colors for categories, reviewed and refined.

## When to use

- Brainstorming ideas solo or with a team
- Planning projects, essays, or presentations
- Studying and memorizing connected material
- Breaking down complex problems visually
- Organizing research before writing

## Core concepts

- **Radiant structure.**
  Central topic in the middle; main branches for key themes; sub-branches for details. Hierarchy reads outward from center.
- **One keyword per branch.**
  Single words trigger associations; phrases turn maps into linear notes wearing a costume. Keywords keep it visual.
- **Visual encoding.**
  Colors per main branch, simple images/symbols, varied branch thickness. Visual distinctiveness aids memory dramatically.
- **Association over hierarchy.**
  Cross-links between branches where ideas connect. The map's power is showing non-hierarchical relationships.
- **Divergent then convergent.**
  First: brainstorm freely, no judging, quantity. Then: prune, cluster, prioritize. Two phases, never mixed.
- **Map types.**
  Brainstorm maps (ideas), project maps (tasks/timelines), study maps (concepts), decision maps (options/criteria). Match type to purpose.
- **Digital vs. paper.**
  Paper: fast, free, tactile — best for quick brainstorms. Digital: editable, shareable, expandable — best for living documents.
- **Review rhythm.**
  Maps decay like notes. Revisit, refine, and convert to action — a map nobody revisits is decoration.

## Practical workflow

1. **Define the purpose.**
   Brainstorm? Plan? Study? The purpose determines the center node and branch logic.
2. **Start with the center.**
   Central image + keyword capturing the topic. Spend a minute making it evocative — it anchors the map.
3. **Brainstorm branches.**
   Main themes as first-level branches, one keyword each. No editing during divergence — capture everything.
4. **Expand and connect.**
   Sub-branches for details; cross-links where branches relate. Colors per main branch; add images/symbols.
5. **Converge.**
   Prune ruthlessly, cluster related branches, star priorities. The map now shows what matters, not just what's possible.
6. **Convert to action.**
   Project maps -> task list with owners/dates. Study maps -> self-test. Brainstorm maps -> shortlist. Maps that don't convert are art.
7. **Share and iterate.**
   Digital maps for collaboration: share, gather comments, refine. Version for major iterations.
8. **Revisit on cadence.**
   Study maps: spaced review. Project maps: weekly. Living maps stay useful; abandoned maps mislead.

## Common pitfalls

- **Phrase branches.**
  Full sentences on branches = linear notes in disguise. One keyword per branch, always.
- **No convergence.**
  Brainstorm maps that never get pruned or prioritized. Divergence without convergence is just noise.
- **Map as final product.**
  Beautiful maps that never convert to tasks, writing, or decisions. The map is a thinking tool, not the deliverable.
- **Over-decoration.**
  Clip-art explosions that obscure structure. Visuals serve memory; when they distract, simplify.
- **One giant map.**
  A single map for an entire project becomes unreadable. Split by sub-topic; link maps instead.
- **Never revisiting.**
  Study maps made once, never reviewed. Spaced revisits are what convert maps to memory.
- **Wrong tool for the job.**
  Mind maps for sequential processes or precise data. Use flowcharts, tables, or docs where linearity matters.
- **Solo-only habit.**
  Missing collaborative mapping for team brainstorms. Shared digital maps capture group thinking live.
