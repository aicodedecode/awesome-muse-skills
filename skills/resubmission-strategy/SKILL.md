## Overview

Most funded grants were once rejected: resubmission is the norm, not the
exception. This skill covers triaging reviewer feedback (what to fix,
what to rebut, what to ignore), writing the resubmission introduction
(the 1-page "what changed" document), deciding how much to revise vs
restructure, and the timeline and psychology of coming back stronger.

## When to use

- Planning a resubmission after a grant rejection or low score
- Writing the NIH-style Introduction to Resubmission (or equivalent)
- Deciding whether to revise, restructure, or start over
- Responding to a "not discussed" (triaged) outcome
- Timing the resubmission: next cycle vs waiting

## Core concepts

- **The score tells you the strategy:** near-miss (just below payline) → targeted fixes; middling score → substantial revision of the weak sections; triaged/not discussed → fundamental restructuring or rethinking — possibly a different mechanism or aims.
- **Reviewer archetypes:** the enthusiast (protect their support), the skeptic with fixable concerns (convert with data), the hostile/mistaken reviewer (rebut politely with evidence, and make the revision robust to their misreading), the vague reviewer ("more preliminary data" — interpret generously and deliver).
- **The introduction document:** typically 1 page summarizing what changed in response to the major criticisms — this is what reviewers read first on resubmission; it must show responsiveness without groveling.
- **Fix, don't just argue:** resubmissions that only rebut without new data or text changes rarely improve — reviewers want to see the proposal change, not just the response document.
- **The "new reviewer" problem:** resubmissions often get new reviewers who never saw the old critiques — the proposal must stand alone, not depend on the introduction for context.
- **Diminishing returns:** most mechanisms allow limited resubmissions (NIH: one resubmission as A1) — each round must be a serious upgrade; don't burn the resubmission on cosmetic changes.

- **Reading the summary statement forensically:** distinguish fatal flaws (fix with new data) from misunderstandings (fix with clearer writing) from differences of opinion (address respectfully, don't capitulate) — each needs a different response.
- **The introduction page:** a one-page (NIH) summary of what's changed — organized by reviewer concern, not chronologically; lead with the biggest changes, not the easiest.
- **New data vs new words:** reviewers reward substantive changes — a major new experiment outweighs ten rewritten paragraphs; budget revision time accordingly.

## Practical workflow

### 1. Triage the reviews (within a week, while fresh)

1. Extract every criticism; sort into: fatal (must fix with new data/text), substantial (fix with revision), stylistic (fix cheaply), mistaken (rebut with evidence), out-of-scope (decline politely with rationale).
2. Identify the pattern: do all reviewers hit the same weakness? That's your revision's center of gravity — fix it first and most visibly.
3. Get an outside read: a colleague who hasn't seen the proposal reads the reviews cold — they'll spot which criticisms are valid and which are reviewer hobbyhorses.

### 2. Decide the revision scope

1. **Near-miss:** keep the structure; strengthen weak aims, add the requested preliminary data, clarify the confusing passages. 4–8 weeks of work.
2. **Middling:** restructure weak sections, possibly replace an aim, add substantial new data. 2–4 months.
3. **Triaged:** fundamental rethink — new aims, new framing, possibly new mechanism (R21→R01, different institute) or a different funder. Don't resubmit the same proposal with new fonts.
4. Sanity check: if the core idea scored poorly on significance/innovation (not just approach), revision can't fix it — rethink the idea.

### 3. Execute the revision

1. Generate the new preliminary data first — it's the longest pole; text revision happens around it.
2. Rewrite weak sections from scratch rather than patching — patched text reads patched.
3. Mark changes clearly (many agencies want changed text indicated); keep a change log mapping each major criticism to its fix location.

### 4. Write the introduction

1. Open by thanking reviewers and stating the revision's headline changes (3–4 bullets max).
2. Address each major criticism: what was said, what you changed, where to find it — concise, factual, non-defensive.
3. For disagreements: state your position with evidence, note the change you made anyway to address the underlying concern (this disarms).
4. Close with why the proposal is now stronger — one confident paragraph.

### 5. Plan the resubmission timeline

1. Work backward from the deadline: new experiments first (longest lead time), then analysis, then writing — most resubmissions fail on the experiment schedule, not the writing.
2. Get fresh eyes before resubmitting: a colleague who hasn't seen the proposal reads the revision cold against the old critiques — they'll catch what you've gone blind to.
3. Decide the venue question early: same study section (reviewers may remember) vs a different one (fresh start, new risks) — discuss with the program officer.

### 6. Quick-reference checklist

- [ ] Summary statement forensically categorized (fatal vs fixable vs opinion)
- [ ] Program officer consulted on scope and responsiveness
- [ ] New data addresses the major empirical critiques
- [ ] Introduction page leads with biggest changes, organized by concern
- [ ] Nothing resubmitted unchanged on the major critiques
- [ ] Strong sections protected; surgery focused on weak ones
- [ ] Fresh-eyes read against the old critiques before resubmission
- [ ] Venue decision made (same vs different study section)

## Common pitfalls

- **Cosmetic resubmission:** new fonts, shuffled paragraphs, same weaknesses — reviewers remember, and scores drop.
- **Arguing instead of fixing:** a 10-page rebuttal with no new data — the introduction should describe changes, not litigate the old review.
- **Ignoring the consensus criticism:** all three reviewers flagged feasibility; you added a paragraph — fix the actual weakness with data or redesign.
- **Overcorrecting to one reviewer:** warping the proposal around a single hostile reviewer's hobbyhorse — weigh all reviews; the enthusiast's support matters too.
- **Missing the window:** waiting so long the preliminary data ages or the field moves — resubmit at the next viable cycle with real improvements.
- **Resubmission fatigue:** sending the A1 out of obligation rather than conviction — if you don't believe it's now fundable, don't submit; rethink instead.
- **Resubmitting without addressing the major critique:** hoping new reviewers won't notice — they often include the old reviewers, and the summary statement persists.
- **Overhauling what worked:** rewriting strong sections while under-fixing weak ones — protect what reviewers liked; operate surgically on what they didn't.
