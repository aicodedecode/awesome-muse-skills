## Overview

Almost every offer is negotiable, and the first number is rarely the last. Yet
most candidates
accept the first offer because negotiating feels confrontational. This skill
reframes negotiation as
collaborative problem-solving: you and the employer both want you to sign, and
the only question is
the package. It covers research, anchoring, scripts for every stage, and the
counter-offer trap.

## When to use

- Evaluating a written offer and deciding whether/how to negotiate

- Answering "what are your salary expectations?" early in a process

- Juggling multiple offers for leverage

- Negotiating a raise or promotion internally

- Handling exploding offers and deadlines

## Core concepts

- - - **Never give the first number if you can avoid it.** "What's your expected
  salary?" → "I'd like
  to understand the full scope first — what's the budgeted range for this role?"
Whoever anchors
  first sets the frame.
- - - **Negotiate the total package.** Base, bonus, equity/RSUs, sign-on,
  relocation, remote
  flexibility, start date, title, review timeline. A "no" on base often becomes
a "yes" on sign-on
  or equity.
- - - **Market data is your leverage.** Levels.fyi, Glassdoor, Pave, peer
  conversations — come with a
  range, not a feeling. "Based on market data for this role in this market, I'm
targeting X-Y."
- - - **Silence is a tactic.** After you state your ask, stop talking. The urge
  to fill silence with
  concessions is the most expensive habit in negotiation.
- - - **Get it in writing.** Verbal promises evaporate. Every agreed term —
  including start dates and
  review timelines — goes into the written offer before you sign.
- - - **The walk-away number.** Decide your minimum acceptable package before
  negotiating. Without it,
  you'll rationalize a bad deal in the moment.

## Practical workflow

1. 1. 1. **Research.** Gather 3+ data points for the role, level, and market.
   Note the full range, not
   just the median — you negotiate against the top of the band, not the middle.
2. 2. 2. **Set your numbers.** Three figures: ideal (ask), target (happy to
   sign), walk-away (decline).
   Keep the walk-away private; it's your discipline tool.
3. 3. 3. **Deflect early salary questions.** Use the range-request script. If
   forced, give a wide,
   research-backed range with the top at your ideal — never a single number,
never your current
   salary (it's irrelevant and often illegal to ask for).
4. 4. 4. **Evaluate the written offer.** Score each component against your
   numbers. Identify the 2-3
   levers with the most upside (usually base + sign-on + equity).
5. 5. 5. **Make the counter.** One consolidated ask, framed collaboratively:
   "I'm excited about the
   role and the team. Based on my research and the scope, I'd be able to sign at
[X base, Y
   sign-on]. Can we get there?" Then silence.
6. 6. 6. **Handle the pushback.** Expect "this is our best offer" (often not
   true), "the band doesn't
   allow it" (ask about sign-on/equity, which often sit outside bands), or a
split-the-difference.
   Respond to each calmly, one round at a time — 2-3 rounds is normal.
7. 7. 7. **Close cleanly.** Once terms are agreed, confirm in writing, thank
   everyone, and stop
   negotiating. Post-acceptance renegotiation burns bridges.

## Common pitfalls

- - - **Accepting the first offer.** The first offer is a starting point by
  design. A polite counter
  almost never rescinds an offer — that fear is the employer's best negotiating
tool.
- - - **Negotiating against yourself.** "I was thinking maybe around X, but I'm
  flexible..." — you've
  just lowered your own anchor. State the ask and stop.
- - - **Revealing your current salary.** It anchors you to your past, not your
  market value. Deflect:
  "I'd prefer to focus on the market rate for this role."
- - - **Only negotiating base.** Candidates leave tens of thousands on the table
  ignoring sign-on
  bonuses, equity refreshers, and remote stipends.
- - - **Using a fake competing offer.** Lies get discovered and destroy trust.
  Real leverage only —
  even "I'm in late stages elsewhere" must be true.
- - - **Exploding-offer panic.** "Sign by Friday or it's gone" is usually
  negotiable: "I'm very
  interested — I need until [date] to make a thoughtful decision." Employers who
truly want you will
  wait a few days.
