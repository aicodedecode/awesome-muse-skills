## Overview

Packaging is a salesperson, a billboard, and a legal document in one — it has
about 3 seconds to win
attention on a shelf or thumbnail, then must communicate clearly, comply with
regulations, and
survive shipping. This skill covers packaging design from shelf strategy and
structural basics
through labeling requirements to print-ready dielines.

## When to use

- Designing packaging for a new product

- Redesigning existing packaging for shelf impact

- Understanding dielines, bleeds, and structural design

- Navigating labeling regulations (nutrition, warnings, claims)

- Preparing packaging files for printers

## Core concepts

- - - **The 3-second shelf test.** At 10 feet (or as a thumbnail), only three
  things register: brand
  block, product identity, and variant. Design the "billboard" layer first;
details are for the
  hand.
- - - **Hierarchy of the pack.** Brand → product name → variant/flavor → key
  benefit → supporting
  claims → legal. Each layer serves a different distance: shelf, arm's length,
in-hand reading.
- - - **Structure affects design.** Box, pouch, bottle, tube — each has panels,
  seams, and viewing
  angles that constrain the design. Get the dieline early; design to the
structure, not onto it.
- - - **Claims and compliance.** "Organic," "natural," nutritional panels,
  allergen warnings, net
  weight placement — regulated differently by category and country.
Non-compliant packaging gets
  pulled from shelves; verify with legal, not just the designer.
- - - **Unboxing is marketing.** The opening experience (tissue, inserts,
  messaging inside the lid)
  turns customers into photographers. Design the inside, not just the outside.
- - - **Sustainability signals.** Material choices and recyclability messaging
  increasingly drive
  purchase. Design honestly — greenwashing destroys trust faster than no claim
at all.

## Practical workflow

1. 1. 1. **Define the shelf context.** Where does it sell (grocery shelf,
   boutique, Amazon thumbnail)?
   Who are the neighbors? What's the category's visual language — and how will
you stand out within
   it?
2. 2. 2. **Get the structure.** Obtain or create the dieline: dimensions,
   panels, glue flaps, folds.
   Mock it up physically (even in paper) — flat designs lie about 3D reality.
3. 3. 3. **Design the billboard layer.** Brand block, product name, variant
   differentiation
   (color-coding system for flavors/sizes). Test at thumbnail size and from 10
feet.
4. 4. 4. **Build the information layers.** Benefits, claims, usage,
   ingredients/nutrition, legal — in
   reading order. Keep the front clean; relegate detail to side/back panels.
5. 5. 5. **Handle compliance.** Required panels, font minimums for legal text,
   allergen and warning
   placement, barcode quiet zones, net quantity rules. Check category-specific
regulations for your
   markets.
6. 6. 6. **Prototype physically.** Print at 100%, cut, fold, and assemble.
   Check: does it stand? Do
   panels align? Is text readable at arm's length? Does the variant read from
across the room?
7. 7. 7. **Prepare printer files.** CMYK, correct dieline on a separate layer,
   3mm+ bleed, outlined
   fonts, spot colors/foils/varnishes specified, barcode as vector. Get a
physical proof before the
   full run.

## Common pitfalls

- - - **Designing flat.** Beautiful flat artwork that fails on the 3D structure
  — text wrapping into
  folds, key art on the glue flap. Always mock up.
- - - **Front-panel clutter.** Trying to say everything on the front. The front
  sells; the back
  informs. Ruthless prioritization.
- - - **Variant confusion.** Flavors/sizes that look identical at a glance cause
  mispicks and returns.
  Variant differentiation must survive the 3-second test.
- - - **Non-compliant claims.** Marketing claims the legal team hasn't cleared.
  "Clinically proven"
  without proof is a lawsuit, not a selling point.
- - - **Unreadable legal text.** Tiny, low-contrast mandatory text that fails
  regulations. Know the
  minimum sizes; they're not suggestions.
- - - **Ignoring the thumbnail.** E-commerce packaging lives as a 200px image.
  If the design only
  works at full size, it's designed for a world that no longer exists.
